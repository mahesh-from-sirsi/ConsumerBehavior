1. Groups: Meaning and Nature

A group is two or more individuals who interact, share goals or norms, and influence one another. A crowd waiting at a bus stop is not necessarily a social group; a student project team normally is.

Core properties

Interaction, shared purpose, norms, roles, status, cohesion and mutual influence. Group membership can be formal or informal; stable or temporary.

Why people join

Belonging, information, identity, status, practical assistance, protection and shared interests.

Consumer effect

Groups shape what products people notice, what they consider acceptable, and what they buy or avoid. A WhatsApp cycling group can influence helmets and bicycles.

Distinctions

Primary groups are close and frequent (family, close friends). Secondary groups are less intimate and goal-focused (professional associations). Formal groups have defined structure; informal groups arise socially.

2. Reference Groups and Influence

A reference group is a real or imagined group used as a point of comparison for attitudes, values or behaviour. A consumer need not be a member of a group to be influenced by it.

Consumer-relevant types

Membership groups (groups one belongs to); aspirational groups (groups one wishes to join); dissociative groups (groups one wishes to avoid); primary and secondary; formal and informal; direct and indirect reference groups.

Three influence mechanisms

Informational: accepting others as credible evidence. Utilitarian/normative: complying for rewards or approval. Value-expressive/identification: adopting a group’s identity or values.

When influence is stronger

Publicly visible products; uncertainty or high perceived risk; strong group identification; expert/credible members; close contact; product relevance; group norms; desire for approval.

Marketing application

Identify a credible reference group, match the product and context, choose trustworthy local voices, show real usage, and measure behavioural outcomes rather than follower counts.

Ethical limitation

Avoid fabricated testimonials, undisclosed sponsorships and pressure tactics; disclose paid promotions.

3. Family Functions and Decision-Making

Family is both a consumption unit and a socialisation institution. Family influence is important but not uniform: structure, income, culture and life stage change roles.

Functions

Economic support and resource pooling; socialisation; emotional support; lifestyle and consumption habits; care and protection; shared purchase decisions.

Buying roles

Initiator suggests a need; influencer recommends; gatekeeper controls information; decider selects; buyer purchases; payer funds; user consumes. One person may play several roles.

Decision patterns

Autonomic decisions are made largely by one partner; syncratic decisions are jointly made; husband-dominant and wife-dominant patterns are context-dependent descriptions, not rules.

Husband-wife dynamics

Influence varies with product expertise, time availability, income, preferences, power, negotiation and norms. Avoid assuming that one gender always controls a category.

Conflict resolution

Discussion, compromise, bargaining, delegation and postponement; marketers should address the needs of all relevant decision participants.

4. Family Life Cycle (FLC)

Family Life Cycle describes common changes in household composition and responsibilities that affect needs and spending. Traditional stage models are useful heuristics, not universal descriptions of all families.

Common stages

Young single; newly partnered/married; young children; school-age children; older children; empty nest; later-life household. Single-parent, multigenerational, unmarried and child-free households require adapted models.

Consumption implications

Young single: mobility and personal products. Young family: childcare, safety and convenience. School-age: education and transport. Empty nest: leisure, health and savings. Later life: accessibility and service.

Use cautiously

Income, region, health, household structure and individual preference may matter more than a stage label. Avoid treating the model as a prediction for every person.

5. Opinion Leadership and Personal Influence

Opinion leaders are individuals whose advice affects others’ decisions in a particular product or topic domain. Personal influence is the interpersonal process through which beliefs, evaluations or actions change.

Opinion leadership dynamics

Problem or uncertainty → advice-seeking → leader’s experience or demonstration → interpersonal communication → evaluation → adoption/rejection → feedback and word of mouth.

Characteristics

Domain expertise, involvement, credibility, accessibility, social connectedness and willingness to share; an opinion leader in phones may not be one in insurance.

Measurement

Self-designating scales (reported advice-giving), sociometric nominations (who others consult), key-informant ratings, and observed digital influence. Compare results and account for response bias.

Market mavens

Consumers with broad marketplace knowledge (where to shop, offers, product information) who enjoy sharing across categories; unlike category-specific opinion leaders.

Marketing strategy

Provide genuine product trials, transparent information, expert access, referral tracking, disclosure of sponsorship and post-campaign measurement. Assess conversions, trust, recall and repeat behaviour.

Creating opinion leaders

Brands cannot manufacture genuine trust instantly. They can educate credible advocates, support product experience, develop communities and let expertise emerge; avoid fake reviews.

6. Diffusion of Innovation

Adoption is the individual’s decision journey; diffusion is the spread of an innovation across a social system over time through communication channels.

Innovation adoption process

Awareness/knowledge → interest/persuasion → evaluation/decision → trial or implementation → adoption/confirmation. Textbooks use slightly different labels; explain the version used.

Diffusion process

Innovation communicated through channels over time among members of a social system. Adoption rates depend on product and context.

Five perceived attributes

Relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability and observability. Greater advantage, compatibility, trialability and observability generally help adoption; greater complexity slows it.

Adopter categories

Innovators (2.5%), early adopters (13.5%), early majority (34%), late majority (34%), laggards (16%) are approximate theoretical categories in the classic normal-curve model, not fixed market quotas.

Practical example

UPI adoption: usefulness, merchant acceptance, ease, visible peer use and trust may affect adoption; digital access and fraud concerns can slow adoption.

Marketing implications

Demonstrate relative advantage, reduce trial barriers, provide onboarding, use credible early users, simplify the experience and address uncertainty.
