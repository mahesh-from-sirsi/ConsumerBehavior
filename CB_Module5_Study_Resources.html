Case studies (6)

Read each fictionalised Indian-market case, answer in teams, then expand the suggested analysis.

Case 1: The ₹42,000 Smartphone Debate

A retailer in Shivamogga notices that two graduates with similar salaries choose very different phones. Asha buys a ₹16,000 handset and saves for a professional certification. Kiran takes an EMI for a ₹42,000 model because his colleagues use premium devices. Both understand specifications and have different family commitments.

Concepts: Social class versus income; status consumption; aspirational reference groups; budget constraints.

  1. Is Kiran necessarily of a higher social class than Asha? Explain.
  2. Which parts of Kiran’s decision suggest status signalling, and what alternative explanations exist?
  3. Design two ethical retail messages for these customers.
Show suggested analysis
  1. No: income and a single purchase do not establish social class.
  2. Status may matter, but camera, ecosystem or reliability may also explain choice. Interview before concluding.
  3. Asha: total value and repair support; Kiran: transparent EMI cost and real product benefits.

Case 2: The Five-Segment Refrigerator Launch

A mid-sized appliance company plans a refrigerator campaign across Bengaluru, Shivamogga and nearby villages. Its team proposes one expensive model and one national slogan. Store interviews reveal different priorities: space, power use, service coverage, design and financing.

Concepts: Lifestyle profiling; five illustrative class categories; segmentation limits.

  1. Why is a five-tier income label insufficient?
  2. Which additional variables should the team collect?
  3. Recommend a product and channel test.
Show suggested analysis
  1. Household size, housing, energy costs and service access matter.
  2. Observe usage, disposable income, power reliability, repair needs and retail channel.
  3. Pilot 2–3 variants and compare total cost, satisfaction and repeat referrals.

Case 3: Festival Campaign That Missed the Audience

A national clothing brand reuses one festive advertisement in Karnataka, Kerala and West Bengal with translated captions. Some viewers say the visuals and timing do not reflect their local celebrations. Others appreciate the product but find the message generic.

Concepts: Culture; values and customs; localisation; transcreation.

  1. Identify the cultural assumptions in the campaign.
  2. What research should happen before redesign?
  3. Which elements should remain standardised?
Show suggested analysis
  1. A single festival calendar, symbols and language register were assumed.
  2. Local interviews, cultural review and message pretests across age groups.
  3. Core brand promise and quality standards can remain consistent.

Case 4: A Breakfast Mix Across Three Regions

A start-up sells a ready-to-cook breakfast mix nationally. Online ads perform well, but repeat orders vary widely. Interviews show differences in morning routines, taste, family acceptance, pack size and cooking equipment.

Concepts: Subculture; usage context; cross-cultural analysis.

  1. Is the difference necessarily caused by religion or region?
  2. Propose a fair comparative research design.
  3. What adaptations would you pilot?
Show suggested analysis
  1. No. Convenience, income, distribution and household size can be confounders.
  2. Matched samples, local-language interviews and comparable usage tests.
  3. Taste, pack size, preparation instructions and retail availability.

Case 5: The Translated Advertisement Backfires

A consumer-goods company translates a humorous Hindi campaign literally into Kannada. The joke sounds rude to some viewers, while the call to action is unclear. The company considers dropping the Karnataka market.

Concepts: Cross-cultural problems; language equivalence; transcreation.

  1. Why can literal translation fail?
  2. How should the company respond and test a revision?
  3. What would count as evidence of improvement?
Show suggested analysis
  1. Humour, idioms and social expectations differ.
  2. Use native creative writers, focus groups and respectful pretesting.
  3. Comprehension, brand perception, complaint rate and conversion.

Case 6: One India, One Price?

A personal-care brand proposes identical premium packaging and pricing across metro stores and tier-2 towns. Retailers report that shoppers want smaller packs and clearer usage instructions, while some metro shoppers want refill formats.

Concepts: Standardisation/adaptation; 4Ps; inclusive marketing.

  1. Which elements should be standardised?
  2. Which elements could be adapted?
  3. How do you avoid stereotyping smaller-town consumers?
Show suggested analysis
  1. Safety, quality and truthful brand positioning.
  2. Pack size, retail mix, language, refill options and service support.
  3. Test actual preferences and avoid assuming all town residents want cheap products.
