Motivation: Needs, Motives, Goals & Theories

Why do consumers want one product rather than another? Learn how needs become motives, how goals direct behaviour, why motivation changes over time, how motives are aroused, and how marketers apply Maslow and McGuire.

Study path: understand the process → distinguish needs, motives and goals → explore motivation dynamics → learn arousal and conflicts → compare theories → apply and practise.

1. What is motivation?

Motivation is the internal driving force that energises and directs behaviour toward a goal. In consumer behaviour, it helps explain why someone searches for, chooses, buys, uses or avoids a product or service.

Need

A perceived deficiency or desired condition.

Example: needing transport to college or work.

Motive

The activated reason or driving force that energises action.

Example: wanting reliable, affordable transport after repeated delays.

Goal

The desired end state or outcome toward which action is directed.

Example: reaching college on time every day.

Want

A particular way a person prefers to satisfy a need.

Example: choosing a bus pass, scooter, bicycle or ride-share.

Remember: a need is not the same as a product. Consumers may share a need but choose different products because of budget, values, experience, culture and personal goals.

Types of motives

DistinctionMeaningConsumer example
Positive motivationMoves toward a desirable outcome.Buying a course to develop a new skill.
Negative motivationMoves away from an unwanted outcome or threat; often linked with avoidance.Buying a helmet to reduce risk of injury.
Rational motiveEmphasises practical criteria such as cost, durability, performance or efficiency.Comparing mileage and service costs before buying a scooter.
Emotional motiveEmphasises feelings, identity, belonging, pleasure or pride.Choosing a gift because it feels meaningful.
Utilitarian needPractical usefulness or task completion.Buying a reliable laptop for assignments.
Hedonic needPleasure, enjoyment, fantasy or sensory experience.Buying a game or planning a special dining experience.

One purchase can involve several motives. A phone can be chosen for battery life (rational/utilitarian), camera enjoyment (hedonic), safety for family contact (avoidance) and social identity (emotional).

2. Motivation process, needs and goals

A useful basic model starts when consumers notice a gap between their current state and a desired state. They form or activate a goal, choose a strategy, act, and evaluate the result. The real process can loop, pause or change direction.

Goal selection

  • Personal relevance: does the goal matter now?
  • Resources: can the consumer afford the money, time and effort?
  • Feasibility: does the chosen route seem likely to work?
  • Competing goals: must the consumer choose between incompatible priorities?
  • Social and cultural context: what do family, peers and norms encourage or discourage?

Intrinsic motivation

Action is driven by interest or enjoyment in the activity itself.

Example: learning photography because taking photos is enjoyable.

Extrinsic motivation

Action is driven by an outcome separate from the activity.

Example: completing a course to earn a credential or qualify for a role.

Promotion focus

Emphasis on hopes, gains, achievement and advancement.

Example: choosing a course to unlock a new career opportunity.

Prevention focus

Emphasis on duties, safety, responsibility and avoiding losses.

Example: choosing insurance to reduce financial exposure.

Motivational conflict

Conflict typeMeaningExample
Approach–approachChoosing between two desirable alternatives.Two attractive holiday destinations within the same budget.
Approach–avoidanceOne option has both attractive and unattractive features.Wanting a premium phone but worrying about its price.
Avoidance–avoidanceChoosing between two undesirable alternatives.Paying for an urgent repair or continuing to manage an unreliable device.
Exam tip: draw the motivation-process flow, define each stage, and use one consistent example throughout the answer.

3. The dynamic nature of motivation

Motivation is called dynamic because its strength, direction and priority can change. A consumer may satisfy one need, develop a new one, pursue several goals at once, or revise a goal when circumstances change.

Needs are never permanently “finished”

Some needs recur, such as food and transport. Others can develop as people gain experience or change roles.

Example: after buying a basic laptop, a student later wants more memory for specialised work.

Needs can be latent or active

A need may exist but not command attention until a trigger makes it salient.

Example: a consumer may not think about a power bank until their phone battery repeatedly runs low while travelling.

Goals change with life stage

Income, family roles, education, health, location and responsibilities alter what feels important.

Example: transport priorities may shift from style to safety and storage when family needs change.

Success and failure influence future motivation

A satisfying purchase can reinforce a choice; disappointment can lead to avoidance or a new goal.

Example: poor after-sales service may make warranty and service access more important next time.

Multiple motives operate together

Practical, emotional, social and safety motives can all influence one purchase.

Example: selecting a family car for space, safety, comfort, status and running cost.

Situation affects motivation

Time pressure, weather, a sale, availability or the presence of others can change which goal dominates.

Example: buying a nearby meal when rushed, even if another restaurant is preferred.

Homeostasis and drive reduction

Homeostasis is the body's tendency to maintain relatively stable internal conditions. When a physiological need creates tension—such as thirst—behaviour may aim to reduce that tension by drinking. This is useful for explaining many basic needs, but not every consumer action is driven by tension reduction.

When consumers seek stimulation

People also pursue novelty, challenge, exploration and excitement. This means motivation is not always about reducing discomfort; sometimes consumers want an optimal level of stimulation or a rewarding experience.

Do not oversimplify: people do not always act logically or satisfy one need at a time. Habit, emotion, social influence, impulse, limited resources and conflicting goals may affect the outcome.

Mini scenario

A student initially buys the cheapest headphones. After online classes and commuting, comfort, microphone quality and battery life become more important. The student’s underlying need for communication existed before, but experience changed the priority of the goals and product attributes.

4. Arousal of motives

Arousal of a motive means a need becomes active or salient enough to influence attention and behaviour. A need may be activated by internal conditions, external cues, emotional states, new information or a consumer’s own expectations.

Source of arousalHow it worksExample
PhysiologicalInternal bodily state activates a need.Thirst makes water more salient.
EmotionalFeelings change what seems desirable or urgent.Feeling excited while planning a celebration.
CognitiveRemembering, planning or recognising a gap activates a goal.Remembering that a laptop battery no longer lasts through class.
Environmental / external cueA product, display, message, smell, price or social cue brings a need to attention.Seeing rain forecast prompts someone to check for an umbrella.
Life event / changed circumstancesA new role or situation creates new priorities.Moving to a new city makes local transport and home essentials salient.

What marketers can do ethically

  • Make a relevant need easy to recognise and understand.
  • Explain how a product could help meet that need, using accurate claims.
  • Make useful options accessible at the time the need arises.
  • Avoid manufacturing panic, exploiting vulnerabilities or exaggerating threats.
Ethical boundary: motivating a consumer is not a licence to manipulate. Fear-based messages should be proportionate, truthful and paired with realistic action—not designed to shame or frighten people into unnecessary purchases.

5. Motivation theories and marketing strategy

A. Maslow’s hierarchy of needs

Maslow proposed categories of human needs often shown as a hierarchy. In consumer behaviour, the model helps marketers think about different benefits consumers may seek. Treat the hierarchy as a useful framework—not a rigid universal ladder that every person follows in exactly the same order.

LevelMeaningConsumer/marketing example
1. PhysiologicalFood, water, rest and basic bodily needs.Food, drinking water, basic clothing.
2. SafetySecurity, protection, stability and reduced risk.Insurance, helmets, secure payment features.
3. Love / belongingRelationships, affection, group membership.Family-oriented services, group activities, community products.
4. EsteemRespect, achievement, confidence and recognition.Professional clothing, achievement awards, premium products used for status.
5. Self-actualisationPersonal growth, fulfilment and realising potential.Learning experiences, creative tools, challenging travel or skill development.
Limitation: people may pursue esteem, belonging or growth while some lower-level needs remain unmet. Culture, income, personality and context affect priorities. Use Maslow to generate hypotheses about motives, not to label every buyer with certainty.

B. McGuire’s psychological motives

McGuire’s framework classifies psychological motives along two dimensions: cognitive vs affective and preservation of the existing state vs growth/change. Consumer-behaviour textbooks may use slightly different labels or examples; the table below gives the common four-part scheme in the syllabus.

CategoryCore ideaCommon motive labels / consumer illustration
Cognitive preservationUnderstand, organise and maintain a coherent view of the world.Consistency: preferring a brand that fits existing beliefs. Attribution: seeking an explanation for why a product failed. Categorisation: grouping products into familiar types. Objectification: using observable evidence or cues to infer less visible qualities.
Cognitive growthSeek information, variety and a broader understanding.Autonomy: wanting independent choice. Stimulation: seeking novelty. Teleological / goal-oriented motives: organising choices around desired outcomes. Utilitarian motives: solving practical problems efficiently.
Affective preservationProtect emotional security, identity or relationships.Tension reduction: seeking relief from discomfort. Self-expression: showing identity. Ego defence: protecting self-esteem. Reinforcement: repeating rewarding experiences. Affiliation: maintaining connection with others.
Affective growthDevelop emotionally, explore identity and build meaningful relationships or experiences.Assertion: seeking achievement or recognition. Affiliation / identification: feeling connected to a group or admired figure. Modelling: adopting behaviours or styles observed in others. Some texts group these motives differently.
How to study McGuire: learn the two axes first, then connect each motive label to the category used in your prescribed textbook. Exact sub-motive lists and translations can vary by edition.

Compare the theories

MaslowMcGuire
Broad categories of human needs arranged in a commonly depicted hierarchy.A set of psychological motives classified by cognitive/affective and preservation/growth dimensions.
Useful for discussing broad benefit themes.Useful for more specific explanations of why consumers seek consistency, variety, affiliation, autonomy or other psychological outcomes.
Marketing question: “Which need may this benefit address?”Marketing question: “Which psychological motive may explain this choice?”

6. Corporate illustrations and case practice

These are examples for applying theories. The examples illustrate possible motives; they do not prove that one motive alone caused a purchase or a company's success.

Case A — Amul and everyday food choices

A family chooses a familiar dairy brand. Possible motives include taste, availability, trust, habit, value for money and family preference. Different family members may prioritise different benefits.

Discuss: Identify one utilitarian need, one emotional or social motive and one external cue that might activate the purchase.

This is an illustrative classroom case; ask students to use their own local product observations rather than assume all consumers share the same motives.

Case B — Insurance and safety motives

A household considers health or vehicle insurance. The choice may be driven by avoiding financial loss, fulfilling family responsibilities and seeking peace of mind. Rational comparison of coverage and emotional reassurance can operate together.

Discuss: Map this example to Maslow’s safety needs. Which evidence would a consumer need to evaluate the product responsibly?

Case C — Learning app or professional course

A learner purchases a course to gain a practical skill, improve confidence, earn a credential or qualify for a new role. The same product can address utilitarian, esteem and self-development goals, with different priorities for different learners.

Discuss: Which motives are intrinsic and which are extrinsic? What might make the motivation persist after the initial excitement fades?

Mini-case — headphones: A commuter wants better audio for a long journey. A low price, noise reduction, comfort, brand image, influencer review and limited budget all matter. Identify the need, motive, goal, possible arousal cue, and one conflict type before recommending a product.

Private application task

Choose a recent purchase (your own or an imagined consumer’s). Map it to need → motive → goal → trigger → action → outcome. Then apply one Maslow level and one McGuire motive.
