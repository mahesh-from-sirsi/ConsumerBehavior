Online Decision Making & Situational Influences

Explore how consumers decide online and how the surrounding situation changes their choices. Learn the concepts, apply them to realistic examples, and test understanding privately.

Learning approach: Before revealing an explanation, predict what you think is happening and why. The aim is not just to remember labels, but to connect each concept to consumer behaviour.
Topic A

Meaning of online decision making

Online consumer decision making is the process of recognising a need, gathering and evaluating information, choosing and purchasing through digital channels, and assessing the experience afterwards. Digital channels include shopping apps, websites, search engines, social platforms and online marketplaces.

Core distinction

The basic consumer decision process remains useful online. What changes is how consumers find information, compare alternatives, interact with sellers, pay, receive products and share feedback.

An online purchase is not automatically high involvement. Buying a familiar household item online may be routine; buying a laptop in a physical shop may require extensive problem solving.

Topic B

Nature of situational influence

A consumer's choice can be affected by temporary circumstances associated with a particular time and place, beyond enduring personal characteristics and the product's own attributes. Russell W. Belk's foundational work organised these influences into five groups: physical surroundings, social surroundings, temporal perspective, task definition and antecedent states.

Reference: Belk, R. W. (1975), “Situational Variables and Consumer Behavior,” Journal of Consumer Research, 2(3), 157–164. DOI: 10.1086/208627.

Personal influence

A relatively enduring or individual factor, such as a person's general preferences, knowledge or lifestyle.

Example: Someone generally prefers simple, durable shoes.

Situational influence

A circumstance at a particular time and place that can change the choice or behaviour.

Example: The same person buys formal shoes because an interview is tomorrow.

Why it matters

The same consumer may behave differently across situations. A situation can affect whether a need is noticed, how much information is searched, which criteria matter, what is purchased, how the product is used and how it is disposed of.

Belk's situational characteristics

Five characteristics and their effects on consumption

These are the five characteristics named in the VTU syllabus. They are a way to classify situational variables, not five mandatory steps in a consumer's decision.

CharacteristicIllustrative examplePossible effect
Physical surroundingsBright lighting, store layout, music, temperature or a website's visual designMay affect attention, comfort, browsing and perceived quality
Social surroundingsShopping with friends, advice from family, sales staff or other shoppersMay shape preferences, confidence, social pressure or choice
Temporal perspectiveShopping just before a deadline, during a festival sale or with little time availableMay change search depth, urgency, purchase timing or willingness to wait
Task definitionBuying a phone for personal use versus buying it as a gift for a parentChanges the criteria that matter and the intended recipient's needs
Antecedent statesArriving tired, stressed, excited, hungry or with limited cash availableMay affect patience, attention, mood and willingness to spend
Important nuance

Situational influence is not guaranteed to affect everyone in the same way. The same deadline may make one shopper buy quickly and another postpone the purchase. Use “may influence” rather than claiming that a factor always causes a particular behaviour.

Misconception clinic

Claim 1:

“Online decision making has a completely different set of stages from offline decision making.”

Too absolute. The common five-stage buying process remains a useful framework. Online environments change the available information, interface, transaction and feedback channels; consumers may also skip or revisit stages.
Claim 2:

“A person's purchase is explained only by their personality and the product's features.”

Incorrect. Situational circumstances such as time pressure, shopping companions, the purpose of purchase and temporary mood or fatigue may also influence behaviour.
Claim 3:

“If a consumer sees an advertisement before buying, the advertisement definitely caused the purchase.”

Incorrect. Exposure alone does not prove causation. The consumer may already have intended to buy, may have been influenced by reviews or friends, or may have acted for another reason.
