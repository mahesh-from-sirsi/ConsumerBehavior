Topic A

Consumer Buying Decision Process

One common textbook framework describes five stages. The framework helps us analyse decisions; it does not mean every purchase follows every stage in a rigid order.

See the whole process in one example

Scenario: a student's phone battery is failing.

StageWhat the student might doUseful question
Need recognitionRealises the phone no longer lasts through a day.What problem or need has become noticeable?
Information searchChecks reviews, asks friends, visits websites or shops.What information can help?
EvaluationCompares battery, price, camera, storage and reliability.Which option best fits my criteria?
Purchase decisionChooses a model and seller; delivery fees or stock may change the plan.What will I actually buy?
Post-purchase evaluationJudges performance, feels satisfied or disappointed, and may recommend or complain.Did the product meet expectations?
Important nuance

Consumers may loop back, skip stages, postpone a purchase or abandon it. Buying toothpaste may involve little deliberate search; buying a costly laptop may involve much more.

Topic B

Levels of Consumer Decision Making

These levels describe the amount of problem-solving effort used for a purchase—not three fixed kinds of people. The same person can use different levels for different purchases.

Compare the levels

DimensionExtensiveLimitedRoutinized response
Consumer's familiarityLow or insufficientSome familiarityHigh, based on experience
Evaluation criteriaNeed to establish or clarify themBasic criteria exist; refine themAlready established
Information searchBroad and relatively intensiveFocused, limited searchLittle or none beyond memory or habit
ExampleFirst laptop purchaseChoosing a different phone brand after comparing a few modelsBuying the usual brand of toothpaste
Marketing implicationProvide useful education, comparisons and reassuranceMake differences and relevant benefits easy to compareMaintain availability, familiarity and consistent experience
Don't confuse these with the five stages.

The five stages describe what may happen during a decision. The three levels describe how much effort and problem-solving the consumer uses.

Topic C

Four Views of Consumer Decision Making

These are four broad perspectives on what drives consumer decisions. They are explanatory lenses, not mutually exclusive categories of people.

Four views at a glance

ViewAssumption about the consumerExampleLimitation
EconomicCompares options rationally to maximise utility, assuming sufficient information and decision ability.Compares total cost, specifications and warranty before buying a laptop.Real consumers rarely know every alternative or calculate every consequence; time and information are limited.
PassiveIs portrayed as largely influenced by marketing and may respond to promotional pressure.Buys because a celebrity endorsement or repeated advertisement is persuasive.Underestimates consumer agency, scepticism, preferences and resistance to persuasion.
CognitiveActively processes information and seeks a satisfactory solution, often using shortcuts.Reads a few trusted reviews and compares two shortlisted phones.Consumers do not always search or reason carefully; feelings and habits matter too.
EmotionalFeelings, mood, identity, affection or symbolic meaning can shape choices.Buys a gift because it feels meaningful, or chooses a team jersey out of loyalty.Not every emotional purchase is irrational; emotions can coexist with practical evaluation.
Core insight

One purchase can involve several views. A buyer may compare prices (economic), use trusted reviews (cognitive), feel attached to a brand (emotional), and still be influenced by an advertisement (passive influence).

Misconception clinic

Read each claim. Decide whether it is sound, then reveal the reasoning.

Claim 1

“Every consumer must pass through all five buying stages in order.”

Incorrect. The stages are a framework. Consumers can skip stages, revisit them, postpone or abandon purchases. The amount of deliberation depends on the consumer and situation.
Claim 2

“A consumer who buys emotionally is never rational.”

Incorrect. Emotion and deliberation can coexist. A person can compare practical options and also choose based on affection, identity, mood or symbolic meaning.
Claim 3

“Extensive problem solving is a personality type.”

Incorrect. It describes decision effort in a purchase situation. The same person may carefully research a laptop but routinely buy their usual groceries.

Quick distinction to remember

Buying decision process

What may happen? Need recognition → information search → evaluation → purchase → post-purchase evaluation.

Levels of decision making

How much effort? Extensive problem solving → limited problem solving → routinized response behaviour.

Four views

How might we explain it? Economic, passive, cognitive and emotional perspectives.

They are not competing lists

A single purchase can be analysed with all three frameworks because each answers a different question.
