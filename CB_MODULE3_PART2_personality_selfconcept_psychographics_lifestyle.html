Personality, Self-Concept, Psychographics & Lifestyle

Understand why two people can buy different products even when they have similar needs. Explore the concepts through visuals, Indian-market examples, corporate illustrations, read-aloud notes, a quiz and exam-answer scaffolds.

Study tip: learn the definition → understand the framework → connect it to a buying example → practise writing the answer in your own words.

1. Personality: meaning and nature

Personality refers to relatively enduring psychological characteristics and patterns that influence how a person thinks, feels and behaves. In consumer behaviour, personality may help explain preferences, but it does not determine every purchase.

Nature / characteristics of personality

Individual differences

People vary in sociability, curiosity, risk tolerance, orderliness and other characteristics.

Example: one buyer enjoys trying a new phone brand; another waits for years of reviews.

Relatively consistent

Some tendencies recur across situations, but behaviour can still change with context.

A price-conscious shopper may still pay more for an urgent medical need.

Develops over time

Biology, learning, culture, relationships and experience may contribute to personality.

A person may become more confident using technology after positive experiences.

Not the only influence

Personality interacts with income, goals, social norms, product category and situation.

An adventurous person may still choose the safest available helmet.

Exam-ready point: Personality is a useful segmentation and interpretation variable, but marketers should not assume every person with a particular trait will behave identically.

2. Major theories of personality

For VTU-style answers, focus on Freudian (psychoanalytic), Neo-Freudian (social-psychological) and Trait theories, plus what each may mean for marketing.

Freudian theory

Sigmund Freud proposed that behaviour can be influenced by unconscious motives and inner conflicts.

Consumer example: someone wants an expensive branded watch (id), checks the budget and long-term needs (ego), and considers whether the purchase conflicts with personal values (superego).

Marketing implication: symbolic, emotional and identity-related motives may matter. These are conceptual interpretations, not a reliable way to diagnose a customer’s unconscious motives.

Neo-Freudian theory

Neo-Freudian thinkers gave more attention to social relationships, culture, anxiety and the need for belonging or recognition.

  • Alfred Adler: striving for competence or overcoming feelings of inferiority.
  • Karen Horney: described broad coping orientations often summarised as moving toward, against or away from people.
  • Harry Stack Sullivan: emphasised interpersonal relationships.

Consumer example: a buyer chooses a particular clothing brand to feel accepted by a peer group, stand out, or avoid unwanted attention.

Marketing implication: reference groups, belonging, individuality and social approval can shape brand meaning.

Trait theory

Traits are measurable dimensions on which people differ. Modern trait approaches often use the Big Five.

TraitPossible consumer tendency (not a rule)
OpennessInterest in novelty and new experiences
ConscientiousnessPlanning, comparing and careful use
ExtraversionEnjoyment of social or stimulating experiences
AgreeablenessCooperative or harmony-oriented preferences
Neuroticism / low emotional stabilityGreater sensitivity to stress or uncertainty in some contexts

Marketing implication: trait scales can support research and segmentation, but require valid measurement and should not be used to stereotype individuals.

Quick comparison

TheoryMain focusUseful marketing lensLimitation
FreudianUnconscious motives and inner conflictEmotional and symbolic meaningsHard to verify a specific hidden motive from one purchase
Neo-FreudianSocial relationships and copingBelonging, status, independenceBroad ideas can be difficult to measure precisely

Common exam trap: Do not confuse personality with attitude. Personality is a broader pattern of characteristics; an attitude is an evaluation of a particular object, person, idea or behaviour.

3. Self-concept and self-image

Self-concept is the collection of beliefs and feelings a person has about who they are. Products can be chosen for practical benefits and for how they fit, express or help shape the consumer’s identity.

ConceptMeaningExample
Actual selfHow I currently see myself“I am practical and prefer simple clothes.”
Ideal selfWho I would like to become“I want to become a confident public speaker.”
Social selfHow I believe others currently see me“My classmates see me as dependable.”
Ideal social selfHow I want others to see me“I want colleagues to see me as creative.”

Related concepts

Self-esteem

How positively or negatively someone evaluates their own worth. It is related to, but not identical to, self-concept.

Extended self

Possessions or places can become part of a person’s identity—for example, a musician’s instrument, a family heirloom or a well-used bicycle.

Multiple selves

A person may express different aspects of identity across roles and situations: student, friend, employee, parent or hobbyist.

Self-image congruence

Consumers may prefer brands whose perceived image fits their actual or ideal self. The match can be symbolic, practical or social.

Marketing example: an outdoor brand may appeal to a consumer’s actual identity (“I enjoy trekking”) or ideal identity (“I want to become more adventurous”). A useful campaign should still make credible product claims.

How self-concept may influence purchase

  1. A person holds beliefs about themselves or who they want to become.
  2. A brand carries functional and symbolic meanings.
  3. The person perceives a fit—or mismatch—between brand and self.
  4. This fit can affect preference, but price, quality, access and context still matter.
Do not assume that a consumer’s possessions reveal their complete personality or social worth. The same product can mean different things to different people.

4. Psychographics and lifestyle

Psychographics describes consumers using psychological and lifestyle-related characteristics—especially Activities, Interests and Opinions (AIO). It helps marketers understand how people live, what matters to them and how they may use products.

AIO elementQuestions marketers might askIllustrative answer
ActivitiesHow do consumers spend time?Walking, gaming, cooking, volunteering, travelling
InterestsWhat attracts their attention?Fitness, technology, local food, fashion, personal finance
OpinionsWhat do they believe or evaluate?Views on sustainability, convenience, value, innovation or local products

Demographics vs psychographics

DemographicsPsychographics
Age, income, occupation, location, educationActivities, interests, opinions, values, motivations and lifestyle patterns
“Who is the consumer?”“How do they live, what do they care about, and why might they choose?”
Example: 22-year-old student in ShivamoggaExample: student who enjoys weekend hikes, values durability and compares price carefully

How psychographic research is done

  1. Define the decision the research should support.
  2. Ask clear, non-leading questions about activities, interests, opinions and relevant values.
  3. Collect responses ethically and with appropriate consent.
  4. Look for meaningful patterns; validate segments with behaviour where possible.
  5. Design and test messages or offerings for the segments.
Important: psychographic segments are research-based patterns, not rigid boxes. Avoid sensitive or invasive inferences, and do not treat an illustrative segment as an established fact about real consumers.

VALS and lifestyle segmentation

VALS is a proprietary psychographic segmentation framework developed by Strategic Business Insights (SBI) for the U.S. market. It groups consumers using psychological traits and resources. In exam answers, explain the purpose of VALS; do not claim that U.S. segment labels automatically describe Indian consumers without validation.

Example segment A: “Active explorers”

Illustrative profile: outdoor activities, interest in travel, opinions favouring durability and practical design.

Possible offering: robust bags, repair information, clear performance specifications.

Example segment B: “Convenience-first planners”

Illustrative profile: busy schedules, interest in time-saving services, opinions favouring predictable costs.

Possible offering: easy reordering, transparent fees, dependable delivery windows.

5. Corporate illustrations and teaching cases

Use these as applications of the concepts. A brand’s positioning is not proof that every customer has the personality or lifestyle described.

Case A — Nike: brand meaning and the aspirational self

Nike’s “Just Do It” platform and athlete-centred storytelling can be discussed through the lens of an ideal self: a consumer may want to see themselves as active, disciplined or sporty. Products can also have functional value, such as fit and performance.

Discuss: Which part relates to self-concept? Which part relates to product performance? What evidence would you collect before claiming the campaign changed buying behaviour?

Source for conceptual context: OpenTextBC — Self and Identity. This is an analytical illustration, not a causal claim about Nike campaign effectiveness.

Case B — Apple: perceived brand personality and identity fit

Some consumers may interpret Apple products as creative, premium or design-focused; other consumers may focus on ecosystem compatibility, accessibility or total cost. These are perceptions that should be researched, not universal facts about every buyer.

Discuss: How does brand personality differ from product features? How could self-image congruence influence preference? What competing explanation might account for the same purchase?

Source for brand-personality framework: Annual Review of Psychology — Psychological Underpinnings of Brands.

Case C — Decathlon: lifestyle and activity-based segmentation

A sports retailer can organise products around activities such as walking, cycling, swimming and camping. This is a useful way to discuss lifestyle and usage needs; it does not mean that all people who do the same activity have the same personality or budget.

Discuss: Is activity-based segmentation psychographic, behavioural or both? What additional information would improve the segment?

This is an illustrative retail analysis; validate any claim about the company’s current segmentation strategy with company-specific evidence before citing it as fact.

Mini-case — choosing a backpack: Three MBA students have the same budget. A trekking enthusiast prioritises durability and weather resistance; a fashion-conscious student prioritises appearance and brand symbolism; a commuter prioritises laptop protection and organisation. Their lifestyles, goals and self-images help explain different priorities—but price and product quality still matter.

Student task (private, no presentation required)

Choose one everyday product—shoes, phone, backpack, scooter, skincare or food delivery. Write down (1) one personality-related tendency, (2) one self-concept dimension, (3) two AIO indicators and (4) one alternative explanation such as price or availability.

6. Quiz, answer practice and revision

Choose an answer for each question, then select Check answers. This is a practice activity, not a graded assessment.

Short-answer practice

1. Distinguish personality and self-concept.

Tip: personality concerns relatively enduring patterns; self-concept concerns beliefs and feelings about who I am.

2. Explain AIO with an example.

Tip: define activities, interests and opinions; apply all three to one consumer profile.

3. How can self-concept affect brand choice?

Tip: actual/ideal/social self → perceived brand meaning → possible fit → choice, alongside other constraints.

7-mark answer scaffold: Explain theories of personality and their marketing implications

  1. Define personality and state why it matters in consumer behaviour.
  2. Explain Freudian theory (id, ego, superego) and a qualified marketing application.
  3. Explain Neo-Freudian emphasis on social relationships and coping.
  4. Explain trait theory and give examples of measurable traits.
  5. Compare the theories and state one limitation.
  6. Conclude that personality informs but does not fully determine purchase behaviour.

10-mark answer scaffold: Discuss self-concept, psychographics and lifestyle in marketing

  1. Define self-concept and lifestyle/psychographics.
  2. Explain actual, ideal, social and ideal social self.
  3. Explain self-image congruence and the extended self with examples.
  4. Explain AIO: activities, interests and opinions.
  5. Distinguish demographic from psychographic segmentation.
  6. Describe the process of creating and validating segments.
  7. Apply to a relevant product or brand and discuss ethical limitations.
Last-minute revision: Freud = unconscious motives and inner conflict; Neo-Freudian = social relationships; Trait theory = measurable differences; Self-concept = how I see myself; AIO = activities, interests, opinions; Lifestyle = pattern of living.
