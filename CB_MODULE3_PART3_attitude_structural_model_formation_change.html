Attitude: Structural Model, Formation & Change

Learn how consumers evaluate brands, how attitudes are formed, and why changing an attitude takes more than showing an advertisement. Includes visual models, Indian-context examples, corporate illustrations, read-aloud notes, quiz and exam-answer scaffolds.

Study sequence: define attitude → learn the three components → understand formation → compare attitude-change approaches → apply and practise.

1. Attitude: meaning and key ideas

An attitude is a learned, relatively enduring evaluation—favourable, unfavourable or mixed—of an object, person, brand, idea or behaviour. Attitudes can influence choices, but they do not always predict actual behaviour perfectly.

Attitude object

Whatever the attitude is directed toward: a brand, product, advertisement, issue or behaviour.

Example: attitude toward electric scooters, not “attitude in general”.

Valence

Attitude may be favourable, unfavourable or ambivalent (mixed positive and negative evaluations).

Example: liking a phone’s camera but disliking its price.

Learned, not necessarily fixed

Experience, information, social influence and repeated associations may form or change attitudes.

Example: a reliable service experience may improve a consumer’s evaluation.

Important distinction: an attitude is an evaluation; a belief is what a person thinks is true; an intention is a plan or willingness to act; behaviour is what the person actually does.

Why marketers study attitudes

  • To understand brand preference and rejection.
  • To identify product attributes consumers value or dislike.
  • To assess reactions to advertising, packaging, pricing or service.
  • To design and evaluate communication—without assuming a positive attitude guarantees purchase.

2. Structural model of attitude: the ABC model

The commonly taught tri-component (ABC) model describes three related components: Affective (feelings), Behavioural/Conative (action tendency) and Cognitive (beliefs and thoughts).

ComponentMeaningConsumer example: electric scooterPossible measurement
CognitiveBeliefs, thoughts, knowledge or perceived attributes“It costs less to run than my petrol scooter.”Agreement with belief statements
AffectiveFeelings or emotional reactions“I feel excited about riding it.”Feeling ratings or semantic scales
Behavioural / conativeAction tendency, intention or reported past action“I intend to test-ride it this weekend.”Purchase intention or behaviour questions
Memory trick: Cognitive = think; Affective = feel; Behavioural = intend/act.

Are the three components always aligned?

No. A consumer may believe a product is healthy (cognitive), dislike its taste (affective), and therefore not intend to buy it (behavioural). The components may reinforce or conflict with one another.

Multi-attribute attitude model (Fishbein-style)

Consumers often evaluate brands across several attributes. A simplified model estimates an overall attitude by combining the belief that a brand has each attribute with the evaluation of that attribute:

Attitude score (simplified): \(A_o = \sum b_i e_i\)

Here, \(b_i\) is the strength of belief that the object has attribute \(i\), and \(e_i\) is the evaluation/importance or desirability of that attribute, depending on the scale used. Scores are meaningful only when the measures and scoring rules are defined consistently.

Worked example: choosing a café

3. Attitude formation

Attitudes develop through several routes. A consumer may form an attitude by carefully evaluating evidence, by emotional associations, by direct experience, or by observing other people.

Direct experience

Using a product or dealing with a service can create strong, personally relevant beliefs.

Example: reliable delivery improves a customer’s attitude toward a grocery app.

Classical conditioning

Repeatedly pairing a brand with a positive stimulus may create a learned association.

Example: a snack brand consistently paired with enjoyable family moments in advertising.

Instrumental conditioning

Consequences after a purchase can influence whether a consumer repeats the behaviour and may shape evaluations.

Example: a useful loyalty reward reinforces repeat visits.

Social learning

Consumers observe family, peers, experts or creators and learn what products mean or how to use them.

Example: watching a trusted mechanic explain how to maintain a scooter.

Information and reasoning

Consumers compare claims, attributes, reviews and evidence—especially for important or expensive purchases.

Example: comparing battery warranty, service network and running cost.

Culture and values

Shared norms and personal values influence which product attributes seem appropriate or desirable.

Example: considering durability, local sourcing or family suitability.

Hierarchy-of-effects patterns

Different purchase situations can involve different sequences. A common high-involvement pattern is learn → feel → do. For a low-involvement or habitual product, behaviour or exposure may come first, with feelings and beliefs developing later. Treat these as explanatory patterns, not universal laws.

Formation vs change: formation is how an attitude develops; change is how an existing evaluation shifts. Some mechanisms—such as new experience or persuasive information—can do either.

4. Attitude change

Attitude change means a shift in a consumer’s evaluation, belief, feeling or action tendency. The best approach depends on why the current attitude exists, how involved the consumer is and whether the message is credible.

Change beliefs about attributes

Provide credible evidence that a brand has a valued attribute or correct a mistaken belief.

Example: publish independent test results on battery range, with test conditions clearly stated.

Change attribute importance

Help consumers consider a relevant attribute they previously overlooked.

Example: explain long-term maintenance cost, not just purchase price.

Add a new attribute

Introduce a meaningful benefit that changes the comparison.

Example: repairability, take-back service or a transparent warranty.

Change overall brand evaluation

Improve product performance, customer service, trust or emotional association.

Example: resolve recurring support problems instead of merely advertising better service.

Use credible communicators

Expertise, trustworthiness and a good fit between source and message can affect persuasion.

Example: a qualified technician explaining safety specifications.

Use experience and trial

Samples, demos, transparent trials or service recovery can let consumers test claims directly.

Example: a test ride before choosing a scooter.

Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM)

Central route: more likely when the issue matters to the consumer and they have the ability to process information. Strong arguments can lead to more durable attitude change.

Peripheral route: more likely when motivation or ability to think deeply is lower. Cues such as a familiar spokesperson, attractive presentation or repeated exposure may matter more. This does not mean the consumer is unintelligent; attention and relevance vary by situation.

Cognitive dissonance and consistency

Cognitive dissonance is discomfort that can arise when beliefs, attitudes and actions conflict. After buying an expensive phone, a consumer may seek reassuring reviews or justify the decision. Marketers can reduce legitimate uncertainty with clear onboarding and support; they should not exploit anxiety or pressure customers.

Barriers to attitude change

  • Strong prior experience or deeply held values.
  • Distrust of the source or perceived exaggeration.
  • Selective exposure: consumers may avoid information that challenges their views.
  • Habit, switching costs, limited availability or social pressure.
  • Weak evidence or a mismatch between the message and consumer needs.
Practical rule: diagnose the reason for the attitude before designing the message. If the problem is poor product performance, fix the product—not just the advertising.

5. Corporate illustrations and case practice

These examples illustrate how the frameworks can be applied. They are not proof that one campaign alone caused a particular attitude or sales result.

Case A — Amul: familiar creative associations

Amul’s long-running topical advertising gives students a way to discuss repeated exposure, brand associations and affective responses. Ask whether a viewer’s favourable reaction to an advertisement necessarily means they prefer the product on price, taste or availability.

Discuss: Identify a possible cognitive belief, affective response and behavioural intention. What research would show whether the ad changed attitudes?

Background: ICMR case catalogue — Amul’s topical advertising. Verify the specific case details before using them as factual evidence.

Case B — Starbucks Rewards: reinforcement and repeat behaviour

A loyalty programme can be used to explain reinforcement: a reward after a qualifying purchase may increase the likelihood of repeat behaviour. But repeat purchase is not automatically evidence of a more favourable attitude; convenience, habit or switching costs may also explain it.

Discuss: Is the programme changing attitude, reinforcing behaviour, or both? What additional data would help distinguish these explanations?

Official programme information: Starbucks Rewards. Programme rules and availability vary by market.

Case C — Electric two-wheelers: evidence-based persuasion

Imagine an EV brand is viewed as expensive and difficult to service. A broad emotional ad may not address the concern. A test ride, transparent running-cost comparison, service-network information and a clear warranty may target the underlying beliefs more directly.

Discuss: Which tactics use the central route? Which use peripheral cues? What conditions must be stated for a fair cost comparison?

This is a hypothetical teaching case, not a claim about a named manufacturer’s performance.

Mini-case — a new café: Students believe the café is costly (cognitive), feel it is welcoming (affective), but do not plan to visit (conative) because it is far away. A discount may address price, but location remains a barrier. Use the ABC model to diagnose the issue before proposing a campaign.

Private application task

Choose a brand or product. Write one belief, one feeling, and one intention. Then propose one attitude-formation influence and one realistic attitude-change strategy.

6. Quiz, revision and exam-answer scaffolds

Select one answer for each question, then click Check answers. This is self-practice, not a graded assessment.

Short-answer practice

1. Explain the three components of attitude.

Define cognitive, affective and behavioural/conative; apply all three to one product.

2. Explain two factors that form consumer attitudes.

Use direct experience, conditioning, social learning, family/peers or information processing.

3. Distinguish central and peripheral routes in ELM.

Explain the conditions, type of persuasion cue and a product example.

7-mark answer scaffold: Explain the structural model of attitude

  1. Define attitude and state its relevance to consumer behaviour.
  2. Explain cognitive component with an example.
  3. Explain affective component with an example.
  4. Explain behavioural/conative component with an example.
  5. Show how components may reinforce or conflict.
  6. Conclude that attitude influences but does not guarantee behaviour.

10-mark answer scaffold: Discuss attitude formation and change

  1. Define attitude formation and attitude change.
  2. Explain formation through direct experience, conditioning, social learning and information processing.
  3. Explain how beliefs, feelings and intentions can interact.
  4. Discuss changing beliefs, attribute importance, adding attributes and improving experience.
  5. Explain ELM central and peripheral routes.
  6. Discuss credibility, involvement, dissonance and barriers to change.
  7. Apply to a brand example and note that actual behaviour depends on situational factors too.
Last-minute revision: ABC = think, feel, intend/act. Formation = how attitudes develop. Change = how existing evaluations shift. ELM = central route (arguments) vs peripheral route (cues). Attitude ≠ intention ≠ actual behaviour.
